## Abstract

### Background

Herpesviruses and other neurotropic viruses have long been investigated as potential contributors to multiple sclerosis (MS), but the literature remains uneven across agents, cohorts, compartments, and disease stages. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) now has the strongest epidemiologic support, while evidence for HHV-6, HSV-1, and CMV is more heterogeneous and may reflect interaction effects rather than isolated causal relationships [3-8,12-18].

### Objective

To synthesize viral epidemiology, neurotropic pathogenesis, coinfection dynamics, autoimmunity, and lesion biology into a single framework that better explains MS heterogeneity than one-virus or one-mechanism models alone.

### Main Concept

We propose a frustrated viral-host landscape model in which latent and reactivating viruses, host susceptibility variants, immune-network couplings, and tissue compartments together define metastable disease states. In this view, relapse, remission, and progression emerge from state transitions in a high-dimensional viral-host system rather than from a single uniform pathogenic pathway.

### Evidence Base

The model integrates epidemiologic and mechanistic work on EBV and other herpesviruses, studies of molecular mimicry and immune dysregulation, clinical observations from CSF and imaging studies, and public resources for host genetics, viral genomics, immune epitopes, and functional genomics [3-18,22-27].

### Conclusion

MS may be more accurately understood as a disease of viral-host state instability in genetically and immunologically susceptible individuals. This framing offers a structured way to interpret contradictory findings, prioritize data integration, and develop testable subgroup-specific therapeutic hypotheses.

## Introduction

Multiple sclerosis is a chronic inflammatory and neurodegenerative disease of the central nervous system marked by demyelination, axonal injury, lesion heterogeneity, and long-term disability [1,2]. Viral triggers have remained central to MS research because they offer a biologically plausible link between environmental exposure and persistent immune dysregulation. Among these, EBV has emerged as the strongest candidate, supported by longitudinal epidemiology, immune profiling, and mechanistic links involving infected B cells and cross-reactive targets [3-8]. HHV-6 remains relevant because of neurotropism, differential behavior of HHV-6A and HHV-6B, and reported links to MS activity, whereas HSV-1 and CMV are more likely to act as modulators, cofactors, or markers of broader immune history than as uniform causal agents [12-18].

The persistent challenge is that association does not map cleanly onto mechanism. Viral exposure is common, MS is comparatively rare, and not all cohorts show the same viral signatures or clinical relationships. The question is therefore no longer whether viruses matter in MS, but how viral exposures, viral states, host genetics, immune circuitry, and compartment-specific biology combine to produce distinct disease trajectories. This manuscript addresses that question by integrating the repository's epidemiology, pathogenesis, coinfection, autoimmunity, lesion biology, and systems-model chapters into a single review-hypothesis framework.

## Section I. Viral Epidemiology and Exposure Architecture

The epidemiology of MS-associated viruses is not best captured by simple seropositivity. EBV has the clearest population signal, including evidence that infection precedes disease risk and that EBV-negative MS is rare [3,4]. However, even EBV likely functions as a necessary but not sufficient factor, because progression to MS appears to require additional host, immunologic, and possibly coinfective conditions [4-8]. HHV-6 adds a second layer of complexity: subtype-specific findings suggest that HHV-6A may be more relevant than HHV-6B in MS risk and neuroinflammation [12-15]. HSV-1 and CMV show weaker and less consistent associations, with CMV in some analyses appearing inversely associated with MS, potentially through immune-regulatory rather than pathogenic effects [16-18].

These data argue for an exposure architecture model with at least five epidemiologic dimensions: (1) order of infection, (2) age at exposure, (3) coinfection combination, (4) reactivation frequency, and (5) population background. Virus-level categories alone are too coarse. A patient with remote EBV infection, recurrent HHV-6A activity, and low-level CMV-driven immune conditioning may occupy a different risk state from a patient with the same named viruses but different timing, strain background, and host susceptibility profile. Future epidemiology should therefore move from binary viral exposure toward combinatorial, longitudinal, and subgroup-aware exposure states [3,4,12-18].

## Section II. Neurotropic Viral Pathogenesis in CNS Compartments

Neurotropic viruses are relevant to MS because they combine latency, reactivation, immune evasion, and the ability to perturb CNS-resident and CNS-bordering cell types. The core pathogenesis framework includes blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, altered endothelial trafficking, viral persistence in neurons or glia, innate immune sensing, astrocytic and microglial activation, and downstream oligodendrocyte injury [1,4,12,18,21]. These pathways do not require continuous high-level viral replication; intermittent or compartmentalized reactivation may be sufficient to sustain local inflammation or antigen release.

BBB dysfunction is particularly important because it sits at the interface between systemic infection history and focal CNS pathology. Viral products, host cytokines, and endothelial stress can amplify transcytosis, adhesion molecule expression, and leukocyte entry, thereby translating peripheral immune perturbation into CNS lesion formation [1,2,18,21]. Likewise, oligodendrocyte injury may occur through mixed mechanisms: direct viral tropism, immune-mediated cytotoxicity, complement activation, and failure of remyelination. These linked processes justify treating viral neuropathogenesis as a compartmented network rather than a single lesion-triggering event.

## Section III. Coinfection Dynamics and Nonlinear Viral Interactions

Coinfection is conceptually important because the immune system rarely encounters one latent virus in isolation. The relevant question is not only whether one virus is present, but how one viral program reshapes the host response to another. Viruses may compete for host-cell resources, alter antigen presentation, suppress interferon signaling, or induce partially overlapping immune responses that amplify tissue injury [12,13,16,18,21]. Such interactions can be antagonistic, cooperative, or sequentially destabilizing.

In MS, nonlinear interaction models are particularly attractive because they explain why common infections can yield uncommon disease states. One viral reactivation event may prime microglia or dendritic cells, another may alter B-cell antigen presentation, and a third may reinforce a chronic cytokine environment that lowers the threshold for autoimmune expansion. This logic aligns with experimental and conceptual work on molecular mimicry, trained immunity, and cumulative antigenic burden [9-11,18,21]. Coinfection should therefore be modeled as an interaction topology, not a checklist of serologies.

## Section IV. Autoimmunity Mechanisms Linking Viruses to Myelin-Directed Injury

The strongest mechanistic links between viruses and MS still come from autoimmunity frameworks. Molecular mimicry remains central because viral peptides can resemble myelin or CNS-associated antigens closely enough to provoke cross-reactive T-cell or B-cell responses [9-11]. EBV is especially important in this regard because both cross-reactive antibodies and EBV-infected B-cell biology provide plausible routes from infection history to sustained CNS-directed immunity [4,5,10,11].

Bystander activation and epitope spreading broaden the model beyond one initiating antigen. Viral infection can create a cytokine-rich environment, activate antigen-presenting cells, and expose new self-antigens after tissue damage, thereby widening the autoimmune repertoire over time [9,11,21]. Once this process is established, direct viral participation may no longer be required at every clinical stage. This helps explain why some studies fail to detect active virus even when viral priming remains biologically relevant. Host genetic background further modifies this risk by altering HLA restriction, innate sensing thresholds, and tolerance stability [6,22].

## Section V. MS Lesion Biology and Clinical-Pathologic Translation

MS lesions reflect the convergence of immune attack, barrier breakdown, cellular stress, and incomplete repair. Viral frameworks are most persuasive when they can be translated into known lesion features, including demyelination, oligodendrocyte loss, clonal lymphocyte expansion, gray-matter involvement, and progressive axonal degeneration [1,2,18,21]. A virus-centered hypothesis that does not connect to actual lesion heterogeneity remains incomplete.

Clinical-pathologic translation also requires attention to timing. Relapses may correspond to transient destabilization of a previously controlled viral-host state, whereas progressive disease may reflect accumulated consequences of prior inflammatory injury, compartmentalized persistence, or repair failure. This distinction is critical: a mechanism that helps explain relapse onset may not be the same mechanism that sustains progression. A multiscale model must therefore accommodate both acute transitions and long-horizon tissue remodeling [1,19,21].

## Section VI. Integrated Viral-Host Systems Model

The central hypothesis of this manuscript is that MS-relevant viral coinfection behaves like a frustrated viral-host landscape. In this model, viruses and viral states form interacting nodes; host variants create biasing fields; immune pathways act as coupling terms; tissue compartments constrain which interactions are possible; and clinical states such as remission, relapse, and progression behave as metastable attractors. The system is frustrated because no single interaction pattern satisfies all competing pressures simultaneously.

This framework differs from additive models. An EBV-associated B-cell axis may increase autoimmune priming, while CMV-associated immune conditioning may dampen or redirect certain responses. HHV-6A may enhance glial vulnerability or inflammatory persistence in one context but have a different effect in another host background [12-18]. The same signaling network may therefore stabilize one patient and destabilize another. Contradictory cohort findings are not noise to be ignored; they may reflect genuine occupancy of different regions in the same biological landscape.

## Section VII. Host Genetics and Viral Susceptibility Architecture

Host genetics must be treated as state-shaping biology, not merely as background predisposition. MS-associated HLA alleles, immune-regulatory loci, and antiviral-response genes can change which viral peptides are efficiently presented, how strongly innate pathways are activated, how stable regulatory T-cell programs remain, and how quickly barrier dysfunction escalates under inflammatory stress [2,4,6,22]. Genetic susceptibility thus affects not only disease risk, but also the reachable and stable states within a viral-host network.

ClinVar is especially useful in this context because it moves the discussion from association to interpreted variant consequence. A ClinVar-guided framework can organize variants into functional classes: antigen presentation, interferon signaling, checkpoint control, endothelial integrity, and viral persistence/reactivation control [22]. When layered onto MS cohorts and viral signatures, this approach supports a more actionable taxonomy of risk than generic gene lists alone.

## Section VIII. Viral Genomics, Epitope Structure, and Comparative Immune Mapping

Strain-aware viral biology is a major missing link in the MS-virus literature. NCBI Virus and ViPR allow investigators to move beyond virus names toward genome- and protein-level variation, while IEDB connects those viral features to experimentally defined immune epitopes [25-27]. This matters because mimicry, immune escape, and antigenic burden are not uniform across all strains or proteins.

Comparative immune mapping can therefore ask higher-resolution questions. Which EBV or HHV-6 proteins carry the most compelling mimicry candidates? Which epitopes are shared across coinfecting viruses? Which strain-specific variations alter predicted HLA binding or cross-reactive risk? By connecting viromic resolution to host restriction and pathway context, the field can move from broad plausibility arguments toward testable, sample-linked mechanisms [10,11,25-27].

## Section IX. Public Datasets and Systems Biology Pipeline

Public MS datasets and host-pathogen resources make this integrative model computationally tractable. GEO and ArrayExpress provide transcriptomic and related functional genomics datasets spanning lesion tissue, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, cerebrospinal fluid, relapse-remission contrasts, and progressive disease comparisons [23,24]. These resources can be combined with ClinVar, NCBI Virus, ViPR, and IEDB to generate aligned matrices spanning host genotype, viral features, pathway activity, compartment, and clinical state [22-27].

The practical requirement is harmonization. Each study sample should be mapped, where possible, to a virus or viral-burden class, host susceptibility class, sampled compartment, dominant pathway signature, and clinical state. Even incomplete datasets can then contribute to a comparative systems framework. This approach also offers a clear research pipeline for the repository itself: literature curation, feature extraction, network construction, subgrouping, and figure generation.

## Section X. Clinical Evidence Synthesis

Clinical evidence in this field ranges from single case narratives to large longitudinal studies. Case reports can be valuable for identifying temporal links between infection, reactivation, neurologic decline, and relapse, but they are hypothesis-generating rather than decisive. CSF studies add compartment specificity but are constrained by sampling time, assay sensitivity, and the possibility that intrathecal immune effects persist after overt viral nucleic acids are no longer detectable [12-18,21].

Imaging and biomarker studies occupy a useful middle ground. MRI enhancement patterns, lesion localization, neurofilament light chain, GFAP, cytokine profiles, and intrathecal antibody signatures can all be interpreted as readouts of state transitions, particularly when they are aligned with viral serology, reactivation markers, or longitudinal symptom data [1,2,19,21]. The goal is not to privilege one evidence type, but to integrate temporal, compartmental, and mechanistic readouts into a coherent clinical picture.

## Section XI. Therapeutic Implications and Patient Stratification

If MS reflects multiple viral-host states rather than one uniform mechanism, therapy should be stratified accordingly. An antiviral-dominant state would be characterized by ongoing or recurrent virologic contribution and might justify suppression-focused strategies. An autoimmune-dominant state would emphasize self-sustaining inflammatory circuitry and may benefit more from immunomodulatory or tolerance-restoring approaches. Mixed states may require combination therapy, whereas progressive neurodegenerative states may demand repair-focused or neuroprotective strategies in addition to inflammatory control [19-21].

This state-based view does not replace current disease-modifying therapy frameworks, but it does suggest a more mechanistic way to interpret why patients with superficially similar diagnoses respond differently. It also provides a structure for biomarker-guided trials in which viral, genetic, and immunologic context are not treated as side notes but as part of the treatment logic itself.

## Section XII. What Is New About This Model

The novelty of this framework lies in its integration. First, it formalizes coinfection as a frustrated-state problem rather than a descriptive coexistence of viruses. Second, it makes host genetics an active component of the model, not a background covariate. Third, it introduces strain-aware and epitope-aware viral interpretation through explicit use of NCBI Virus, ViPR, and IEDB. Fourth, it treats compartment as a primary organizing axis. Fifth, it unifies public omics resources with clinical evidence instead of discussing them separately [4-6,10-18,22-27].

Most importantly, the model offers a principled explanation for contradictory findings. Divergent studies may reflect different viral-host configurations, different compartments, different disease phases, or different population structures. That is a stronger and more productive interpretation than assuming the literature is simply inconsistent.

## Section XIII. Research Agenda and Testable Hypotheses

This review-hypothesis framework leads directly to testable questions:

1. Do high-frustration viral-host states predict relapse better than single-virus serostatus?
2. Do specific host variants bias HHV-6A, EBV, or HSV persistence and reactivation behavior?
3. Do compartment-specific viral signatures correlate with lesion class, enhancement status, or progression stage?
4. Does shared epitope burden across coinfecting viruses predict broader autoimmune repertoires?
5. Do combined antiviral and immunomodulatory regimens perform best in identifiable biomarker-defined subgroups?

These questions are tractable if cohorts, public omics resources, viral databases, and curated literature are linked within a common analytic structure [22-27].

## Discussion

The major strength of the current MS-virus field is that it now contains both compelling population-level evidence and increasingly plausible mechanistic bridges. The major weakness is that these evidence streams are often interpreted in isolation. Epidemiology is discussed apart from epitope structure; viral genomics apart from host genetics; clinical relapses apart from systems biology; and lesion biology apart from data architecture. This fragmentation makes the literature appear more contradictory than it may truly be.

The frustrated viral-host landscape model is intentionally integrative and explicitly hypothesis-generating. Some elements, such as EBV association, are strongly supported [3-8]. Others, such as the exact role of specific coinfection states or the best host-variant classifiers, remain speculative and require direct validation [12-18,22-27]. The value of the model is therefore not that every component is already proven, but that it provides a coherent structure for discriminating established knowledge from high-value uncertainty.

## Conclusion

The central advance proposed here is not another single-virus explanation for multiple sclerosis. It is a multiscale model in which viral exposures, latent/reactivating states, host susceptibility variants, immune-network couplings, and tissue compartments interact to produce distinct disease trajectories. By framing MS as a problem of viral-host network instability, this manuscript provides a clearer path for evidence integration, subgroup discovery, and mechanism-guided therapeutic research.
